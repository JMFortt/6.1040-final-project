# F2: Problem Framing

## Domain

<!-- (an explanation of the domain, or general area, in which the problem lies) -->

Many people learn to draw outside of attending formal art school. To improve, they need to practice their fundamentals, which are the underlying skills that many kinds of drawings depend on, including perspective, proportions, anatomy, lighting, color, composition, etc. Practicing fundamentals usually means drawing from reference images regularly. Without having a teacher or class, the learner is responsible for covering everything that a class would normally provide; this includes choosing what to practice so that they are well-rounded in the fundamentals that they practice, finding references that allow them to practice specific fundamentals, staying consistent with their practice, and judging their own progress. 

## Bad situations

<!-- (one or more bad situations that arise in the domain and that call for a design intervention) -->

1) Self-guided practice tends to drift toward one’s comfort zone, meaning that weaker fundamentals get overlooked whereas stronger fundamentals are preferred. For example, an artist who enjoys drawing faces may draw faces every session because it feels rewarding. But since perspective and lighting feel frustrating to draw, and they feel that they aren’t as good as these skills when compared to faces, they may skip practicing these fundamentals. Months later, the artist’s drawings of faces are extremely strong, but every background looks flat and they can’t say the last time they practiced perspective. Nothing keeps track of which fundamentals they have practiced, so they are unaware of the imbalance until it shows up in their work. 

2) Finding a good reference picture takes away valuable practice time. An artist sits down with 30 minutes of free time to practice lighting. They spend the first 20 minutes searching Pinterest and image sites for a photo with clear, simple light and shadow, but they find mostly finished artwork and cluttered photos, so they end up giving up or settling for an unideal reference. Most reference tools organize the images by subject (hands, animals, portraits, etc), not by the actual fundamental that the image is good for practicing. 

3) Many structured art courses (e.g [drawabox.com](https://drawabox.com/)) suggest that artists practice fundamentals through repetitive and monotonous tasks, such as drawing hundreds of cubes in a vacuum to practice perspective. While these routines are useful for beginner artists, they are often frustrating and unrewarding for intermediate artists who want to hone their fundamentals while still creating artwork that they’re proud of.

4) Practice routines tend to vanish after a setback or pause. An artist starts a daily practice challenge, and keeps it up for two weeks, but they miss a few days during a busy week. Looking back at older drawings feels discouraging and feels discouraging, so the habit loses momentum and they stop practicing fundamentals. 

4) When artists practice alone, they can’t see how others approach the same fundamental/problem and can’t learn from others. After completing their daily practice, an artist wants to know whether their result is reasonable and what they could do differently to improve their drawing. Social media only shows polished, finished pieces drawn with different tools and subjects, so comparing against these drawings is discouraging and uninformative. It is hard to tell whether the difference is as a result of skills, tools, time spent, or the reference itself.

## Workarounds and comparables

<!-- (workarounds that people currently use to mitigate the bad situations, and comparable solutions if they exist) -->

### Workarounds: 

- Pinterest board and self-curated reference folders. Artists save references over time, organizing folders by subject, mood, or fundamentals. Building these folders takes time away from drawing. This process has become especially labor intensive with the increase of AI-generated images on Pinterest that make it harder to find compelling references.
- Self-imposed challenges (e.g. Inktober). Month-long daily drawing challenges provide a schedule, but no structure for what to practice, and they end abruptly. 
- Online groups of artists on platforms like Discord and Reddit. Members post art for feedback, but each person draws different subjects with different tools so comparisons can be hard.

### Comparables: 
- [Quickposes](https://quickposes.com/en): timed reference sessions where references are organized by subject with no record of what you practiced and no community around the shared references. 
- [30 Desk](https://30desk.art/): provides the same reference photos to all users that log in on the same day and challenges them to replicate it within thirty minutes. Does not sort reference photos by fundamentals or track long-term progress.
The main gap is that none of these combine references organized by fundamentals, a record of which fundamentals you’ve practiced, and a shared set of references that a group of artists all draw from to compare results and processes on the same problem. 

## Solution sketch

<!-- (an outline of a solution) -->

Redraw is a practice app where artists draw from references chosen for specific fundamentals using the simple in-app drawing tool. Artists can pick or be suggested a neglected fundamental, draw from a reference in the app’s fundamental bank, add the finished drawing and time-lapse, post them to that reference’s community board, and track their coverage of fundamentals and streak. 

### Concepts: 
- ClassReferenceBank: Give all users that are active in the same time frame (e.g. a day) the same set of reference photos to practice with. These references are tagged with the fundamentals that it would exercise, allowing users to practice specific skills. The “Class” in the concept name refers to art classrooms where students practice drawing the same references to learn from each other and build community through shared struggle.
- CoverageTracking: Keep track of which fundamentals an artist has practiced and which fundamentals are neglected. 
Drawing: Provide a basic drawing tool within the browser that records the artists’ strokes for time-lapses.
- Posting: Sharing drawings of a reference with others drawing the same reference. 
- StreakTracking: Encourages consistent, daily practice. 
- Authenticating: Identify users and facilitate login and sign-up. 

## Ethical analysis

<!-- (an identification of stakeholders and the impacts they might experience in the short and long term. See instructions) -->

### Stakeholders: 
Learning artists: hobbyists, students, and aspiring artists, including beginners. 
Art educators: the practice that Redraw offers can complement their teachings. 

### Insights:
- AI features: we’ve considered using AI to classify images into different fundamental categories, but many of the primary stakeholders (artists) are against AI out of ethical concerns. We’ve decided that sorting photo references by hand would be more appropriate for our target audience.
- Content moderation: although we aren’t planning to allow arbitrary image uploads, the nature of the drawing editor could allow anyone to distribute harmful and illegal content. This is especially concerning in the long-term once we complete the project and stop reviewing drawings regularly. We think the best course of action is to include a report feature and then disable uploads after no moderators (us) are available to review them.
- Responsibility over art: if our app were to become widely adopted, our team would be responsible for maintaining the database of drawing submissions and keeping it accessible for users. We plan on adding a feature for users to download all of their submission data for preservation purposes. Notably, this feature will not allow users to download all images on the website in order to prevent scraping for nonconsensual AI training.
- Mental wellbeing: the social/sharing aspect of our app allows users to compare their art with other users, which can be discouraging and intimidating to beginner artists. To mitigate this, we are adding timelapses as a core feature in our drawing editor so that beginner artists can see the entire process behind an artwork and frame it as a learning tool rather than a comparison.
- Image sourcing: while webscraping is a common technique for finding large banks of images, it has become controversial among artists due to its association with AI training and consequently art theft. To address these concerns, we will be sourcing images only from explicitly public domain datasets (e.g. creative commons 0 licensed photos on Wikimedia commons) and ensuring the image authors are credited.
